Tarr-Coyne Wild West Playground: The Not-So-Wild West NEW!

Most Recently Reviewed On Saturday, October 24, 2026 | Filed under: Best for Tots
A wood-and-sand playground on Central Park's west side that promises the Wild West and delivers a few log stockades. Small-scaled, quiet, and best for ages 2 to 5. The real star is the little river down the middle: great for floating things when it's on, and racing trucks when it's dry. Read the full review »
The Stump Report
Overall Fun:The canal is great, but everything else is a little small.
Originality:The Wild West theme is mostly gone. Points for the novel tin-can chains.
Cleanliness:No trash on the ground, but a few patch jobs and missing swings.
Size:About 17,000 sq ft. Mid-size, but everything in it is scaled down.
Dad Test:OK for dads. Nothing claustrophobic, just nothing big, either.
Bathrooms:None. The mausoleum doesn't count.
From the full review

I was very disappointed that the Wild West Playground isn't actually Wild West themed. There are some round log stockades, and that's about it. I looked up pictures from before the 2015 renovation, and it used to look much more Western. That's a shame. At least there's almost no plastic. It's nearly all wood, and I like that.

It's one of many playgrounds along the west side of Central Park, near the West 93rd Street entrance, and one of several named after and funded by the Tarr family (see also Tarr-Coyne Tots and Tarr Family). Fun fact: the original 1988 design was by Richard Dattner, the same architect behind Adventure Playground.

The equipment is all a little… small. This playground is best for small kids, ages 2 to 5.

There are lots of chains, and I'm not sure what that's about. The tin-cans-on-a-chain thing is unique; I haven't seen one before. It's not all that fun, but at least it's novel.

The river down the middle is great. Kids love floating things down it. It's less a river and more a canal… which is exactly what they need at the Erie Canal Playground! Sure, it's turned off for half the year, but when it's dry it's fun to race trucks down. Fun for me, at least.

There's only one entrance, which gives you great visibility of the whole playground. You can sit on your butt and not worry. It's clean, too: no trash on the ground, just a few patch jobs and some missing swings.

Expect locals, and a private birthday party or two in a corner. It's very quiet during the week, somewhat busy on weekends, and never packed.

No bathroom. There's a little structure at the entrance that looks like a bathroom, but it's not. Maybe it's a mausoleum, because that's exactly what it looks like. Or one of the mole people's entrances to the underground tunnels.

Verdict: a quiet, wooden playground for the little ones, with a great canal. Just don't come expecting cowboys.

Erie Canal Playground: The Knock-Off River Run

Most Recently Reviewed On Sunday, October 18, 2026
A '90s playground in a park with a seedy past. It has a sus rock, sticky mules, slippery floors, and a track ride I haven't seen since I was a kid. Surprisingly nice under the trees, but skip the splash pad and the bathrooms. Read the full review »
The Stump Report
Overall Fun:The large park, the rock, and slippery tag make up for a lot.
Originality:A rare track ride and a sus rock, but the canal theme is mostly a painted line.
Cleanliness:Sinkholes, sticky mules, and trash-planters.
Size:About 15,000 sq ft (the whole park is over 6 acres). Mid-size, in a big park.
Dad Test:It’s a ’90s time capsule; quality nostalgia for dads.
Bathrooms:So close, but so gross.
From the full review

First, the names. The park is DeWitt Clinton Park, on the far West Side of Hell’s Kitchen, and the playground inside it has its own name: The Erie Canal Playground. The Erie Canal was Mayor/Governor DeWitt Clinton’s magnum opus. The mules (not horses) are a canal reference, since mules towed the barges. But apart from three mules and a painted canal route on the ground, there’s really nothing canal-y about this playground. Don’t expect it to be like River Run – it only has a painted stream, instead of a real one.

The park even had a shot at naming the whole neighborhood: there was a push to rename Hell’s Kitchen “Clinton” after it, which never fully stuck, maybe because the park was so nasty back then. For decades it was notorious for being a late-night pickup spot for mixed-gendered people of the night, with drivers pulling in straight off the West Side Highway. It’s a very large park in a commercial part of town, so of course stuff went down here. These days it isn’t all that seedy anymore. People (with kids) are moving into the nice new condos overlooking the Hudson, and plenty of them use this as their neighborhood playground.

And now for the important part, the playground:

Surrounded by London plane trees on a rocky hill, with partial Hudson River and Midtown views, it’s actually kind of nice for a playground in a park with this kind of history.

It was built in 1996, and it’s held up… mediocrely. There are a couple of sinkholes with barricades around them. The original paint is gone or faded, with very poorly done layers of paint slathered on top. The sad mules got the same treatment, and they look sticky. Put your kid on them at your own risk; they may not unstick.

The rubber floor pads are so slick that kids are always slipping and sliding. It makes for an interesting game of tag, and since they’re rubber, falling isn’t all that dangerous, but be warned.

The rock outcrop is a lot of fun, like a mini Umpire Rock at Heckscher. It’s real rock, but it’s been skim-coated in concrete, probably to smooth out the rough or crumbly parts, so it looks just a little off. That’s why we call it the sus rock. No broken glass to be found, so it’s good fun.

I love the metal track ride. I hadn’t seen one in years, and it was my favorite part of my playground as a kid. It might just date the place, but it’s a pretty unique feature, and I had fun on it. As kids we used to purposely crash into people with it. We called it the Crash ’n’ Burn. I was such a jerk...

The actual splash pad is outside the playground, near the bathrooms. There’s usually a lot of riffraff around it from the basketball court and playing fields, so it’s probably best avoided. The fields get very rowdy with adults; expect the kind of soccer game with cases of beer lined up along the fence.

There are public restrooms right outside the playground doors, but they’re some of the scuzziest I’ve ever seen.

Verdict: worth a stop if you’re in the area, or you need a track ride, or you want to get stuck to a mule. We biked here on the Stumpjumper; it’s just across 12th Avenue from the Hudson River Greenway and right near the new Pier 97, which makes for an interesting old-vs-new comparison.

Tarr Family Playground: The Racetrack

Most Recently Reviewed On Saturday, October 17, 2026
Looks pretty ordinary at first glance, but there's a spider web, a little jacks maze, a bridge made for climbing, and a swirly path around the whole thing that kids on bikes treat like a racetrack. Read the full review »
The Stump Report
Overall Fun:A solid choice for new cyclists and climbers.
Originality:Unique features: the swirly racetrack, the spider web, and the jacks maze.
Cleanliness:Very clean. We don't talk about the murder tunnel.
Size:17,000 sq ft. Mid-size, with enough room to zoom around.
Dad Test:The net climber is fun for a dad. The bike bridge will decapitate a dad.
Bathrooms:None nearby; it's a hike to the nearest park restrooms.
From the full review

Tarr Family Playground sits on Central Park's West Side at 100th Street, and it's very easy to get to from Central Park West or the park loop. At first glance it looks like a pretty common playground. Look a little closer and there are some fun features: a spider web, a little jacks maze, and a bridge with lots of ways to climb over it. Some descriptions call the wooden structure a treehouse, but it's a far cry from a treehouse. It's fun, and it's partially made of wood, but that doesn't make it a treehouse.

I call this one the racetrack. A path wraps around the whole playground with a very fun swirl that takes kids down, around, and under a bridge, and kids on bikes love it. Keep an eye on toddlers, since the bikes are usually zooming around. If your kid is a little bigger, check that they can actually fit under the bridge. I've seen some head-bangers.

It's well laid out, with one entrance and exit and good visibility of the whole playground, except in the murder tunnel. The murder tunnel has blood stains. We just pretend it's a volcano and that's lava.

The playground first opened in 1973 as an adventure-style design, and the equipment is stamped 2008, right around when the Central Park Conservancy rebuilt it. It's very clean, it doesn't get too busy, and it usually has a nice crowd of upper UWS locals. The Tarr name is on three Central Park playgrounds (this one, Tarr-Coyne Tots, and Tarr-Coyne Wild West), so somebody in that family really likes playgrounds.

There are no bathrooms nearby that I know of. It's a bit of a hike to the nearest public restrooms in the park.

Verdict: not worth a trip, but not a bad playground by any means. If you live nearby or you're already running the loop, it's a solid stop.

Ancient Playground: The Best Game of Tag in the City

Most Recently Reviewed On Sunday, October 11, 2026
Tucked into a traffic triangle next to the Met and built like a brutalist parking garage. Also, the single best place in New York to play tag. Read the full review »
The Stump Report
Overall Fun:Unbeatable for tag and climbing, just okay for everything else.
Originality:No plastic, but the drab concrete and the obelisks feel phoned in.
Cleanliness:Usually clean, but the floor is patched and the rings are missing.
Size:Approx. 22,000 sq ft, measured on Google Maps. Mid-size, about the same as Adventure Playground.
Dad Test:Dads are officially allowed in the tag games.
Bathrooms:A private bathroom inside the playground, with a side just for kids.
From the full review

Ancient Playground has an awkward address. It sits in the traffic triangle where the 86th Street Transverse meets Fifth Avenue, right beside the Met. You can see traffic on every side and hear the horns while you play. You also can't get in from the park; you need to walk in from Fifth Avenue after playing some pretty hairy Frogger.

The name oversells it. “Ancient” makes you picture pyramids and temples. What you get is a lot of concrete and brick in a drab, brutalist style, plus two concrete obelisks that are apparently meant to go with that other obelisk in the park, the one Egypt didn't exactly hand over. The theming just feels a little phoned in. The toddler area is a sandpit with an awkward concrete slide thing under one of the obelisks. I do like that there's basically no plastic, apart from two tire swings.

But here's the thing: this is the best playground in the city for playing tag. I've been pulled into games with the bigger kids more than once, and I can bound onto and over the pyramids, but I get destroyed in the tunnels. The park is full of little structures that kids climb or run up, then slide or jump down the other side, and some of them have secret tunnels. Heckscher and Adventure Playground have similar structures, but these are exactly the right size for tag: regular, freeze, or the-ground-is-lava.

It's also great for climbers. My daughter named the saggy climbing rope the “bonky rope,” because kids flop around on it and bonk their heads. Overall this playground has held up fairly well, which is the upside of building with concrete, brick, and stainless steel. The trapeze rings have gone missing, and the rubber floor has a lot of patches.

It's usually clean, and it has its own private bathroom with separate Boys and Girls entrances inside the playground. IRL it's not nearly as haunted-looking as in this picture.

Verdict: worth a stop if you're going to the Met (which has a kids' playroom I'll review soon!) or if your kid loves to climb. Otherwise it isn't really worth a special trip. It's great for tag and just okay for everything else.

Swedish Cottage Marionette Theatre: Little Red's Hood

Most Recently Reviewed On Saturday, October 10, 2026 | Filed under: Kid-To-Do Interludes
A real, live marionette show in a 150-year-old schoolhouse in the middle of Central Park. The strings are visible, the theater is tiny and old-fashioned, and the wolf loves cupcakes. Read the full review »
The Verdict
Worth It?A charming, old-school show that held a 3½-year-old's attention, even with a late start and a canned soundtrack.
From the full review

This one isn't a playground, but it's a kid activity worth knowing about. The Marionette Theatre lives inside a very cool old building, the Swedish Cottage: a model schoolhouse built for the 1876 World's Fair in Philadelphia that has been put to all sorts of uses in Central Park over the years. It just got a new cedar shingle roof, which smelled great. Unfortunately, the outside was covered in scaffolding, so you'll have to take my word on how charming it is.

We saw Little Red's Hood, an updated Little Red Riding Hood. Red's a city kid addicted to her phone, Grandma's a spunky visitor to NYC, and the wolf is some kind of escaped lab animal who loves cupcakes. Familiar, but with a twist – like a cupcake. Or Toy Story 5.

It's a real, live marionette show, which is pretty cool. You can see the strings, and the theater is small and old-fashioned in the best way. The show runs about half an hour, and at the end the puppeteers do a little demonstration of how the marionettes work. The one strange part is that all the lines and songs are pre-recorded, so there's a disconnect between the live movements and the canned soundtrack. This is my biggest gripe about the whole thing. It just didn't really feel like live theater without real human voices.

Most kids in the audience were between 3 and 7. There's mixed information out there on whether little kids are free, but I can confirm infants don't need a ticket. My 3½-year-old enjoyed it a lot. Out of about 30 kids, 3 cried and had to be taken outside, which seems like a pretty normal rate for a dark room and a wolf.

Apart from the pre-recorded audio, here's my one real complaint: it started almost 15 minutes late. I showed up 15 minutes early expecting a prompt start, so we sat around twiddling thumbs for a very long time. At least we got the best seats.

The ticket website says there's no stroller parking, but there actually is, right inside the building. There are bathrooms too, so logistics are easy.

Verdict: a sweet, old-fashioned outing that's a nice break from the playground. Just bring a snack for the wait.

Domino Park: Sweet Stuff on the Waterfront

Most Recently Reviewed On Tuesday, October 6, 2026 | Filed under: Worth the Trip & Most Fun for Parents
Playgrounds right on the water with skyline views, clubhouse structures styled after the old sugar refinery, a mist bath that seems to rise from the rocks, and a splash pad with jets that fire at random. Spotless bathrooms, a farmers market some days, and lots for parents to do. Very high marks. Read the full review »
The Stump Report
Overall Fun:Clubhouses, slides, mist, and splash jets. Nobody wanted to leave.
Originality:A playground designed around sugar refining. Brilliant.
Cleanliness:Immaculate grounds, and bathrooms you'd actually use.
Size:About 6,500 sq ft at ground level. Compact footprint, but it climbs several stories, with more play along the water.
Dad Test:Mist bath, skyline views, a big lawn, and a farmers market.
Bathrooms:Across the street, but big private stalls and spotless.
From the full review

Domino Park stretches along the Williamsburg waterfront where the Domino Sugar Refinery once stood, and it has a series of playgrounds right on the water with amazing views of the Manhattan skyline. The equipment is a little different from what you'll see elsewhere; it blends right into the industrial look of the old refinery. The main playground, by artist Mark Reigelman, follows the sugar-making process: kids go in as raw sugar cane and come out the other end as sugar cubes. My daughter did not know that, and did not need to.

The clubhouse structures are tall and enclosed, so visibility for parents is low. There's only one entrance, though, so there's nothing to worry about. Except, possibly, the spider-web net inside a mock industrial vacuum, where a kid can get stuck. Pro tip: there's a way to fish a kid out through the bottom. I know because I had to do it.

Don't miss the mist bath along the water, where a cloud of fog seems to rise up from waves crashing on the rocks below. On a hot day it's magic. The splash pad has jets that fire at random, and kids love tearing through them trying to guess which one goes next, while parents enjoy a big seating area right alongside.

The bathrooms are a bit of a walk across the street, but they're large individual stalls and they're spotless. There's plenty for parents to do nearby, and some days there's even a farmers market.

Verdict: very high marks. Worth the trip from anywhere in the city.

Adventure Playground: No Plastic Allowed

Most Recently Reviewed On Tuesday, September 29, 2026 | Filed under: Hidden Gems
A secluded, sixty-year-old classic on a Central Park hill, built from stainless steel, concrete, and sand — without the modern plastic equipment. A long fire pole, hidey castles, a steep little slide, and an epic ash tree. A smaller, quieter Heckscher that locals love. Read the full review »
The Stump Report
Overall Fun:A good all-around playground: fire pole, hidey castles, and a little river.
Originality:A classic 1966 design with zero plastic, but basically a smaller Heckscher.
Cleanliness:Held up remarkably well for a sixty-year-old playground. Respectful parents.
Size:Approx. 20,000 sq ft, our estimate. Mid-size: a smaller-scale Heckscher.
Dad Test:The long fire pole is fun at any age. Dads fit.
Bathrooms:A bit of a hike, at Tavern on the Green.
From the full review

Adventure Playground sits on a secluded hill near Central Park's West 67th Street entrance, just up the path from Tarr-Coyne Tots and a short walk from Tavern on the Green. Because it's tucked away, crowds and tourists don't wander in; it's mostly local Lincoln Square and southern Upper West Side families. Think of it as a smaller, quieter Heckscher: similar equipment, smaller scale.

It has a great backstory. The plaque says it was built in 1966, designed by architect Richard Dattner as one of New York's very first “adventure playgrounds.” It only exists because of a fight: in 1956, a group of Upper West Side moms stood their ground against Robert Moses, who wanted to cut down trees here for a Tavern on the Green parking lot. The moms won, and this corner of the park got an adventure playground instead. Good job, moms.

I love that there's no modern plastic equipment at all. It's stainless steel, concrete, granite, and sand, which is probably why it has held up so well for sixty years. Sure, technically there is a modern & safe rubber ground mat throughout the playground, but I'll let that… slide. Some noteworthy play pieces: a long fire pole (yes, fun for dads too, as demonstrated by my dad-bud), hidey castles, a very steep short and wide slide, and a little river running from the splash pad to the little series of drains.

It's a very nice secluded spot on a hill with mature trees all around, including an epic ash tree. There aren't many ash trees left of this size. The shade is lovely, but for some reason this place is mosquito central in summer. Bring bug spray.

On weekends, expect to see a birthday party or two. The inside scoop is that the city doesn't hand out fines for setting up small tables here the way it does at some higher-visibility spots.

The nearest bathrooms are a bit of a hike down the hill, attached to Tavern on the Green.

Verdict: nothing really exceptional, but a good all-around mid-size playground. I wouldn't travel far to visit it, but it's understandably a local favorite.

Heckscher Playground: The OG, but Not Necessarily the GOAT

Most Recently Reviewed On Saturday, September 26, 2026 | Filed under: Worth the Trip
Known to regulars simply as “The Heck,” this is Central Park's oldest and largest playground, and the default meetup spot for half the families we know. Castle-like stone structures, a stream you can follow up to Umpire Rock, and skyscraper views. Usually packed with tourists and litter, but still very much worth a visit. Read the full review »
The Stump Report
Overall Fun:Castles, a stream to follow, and room to run.
Originality:Stone castles with a stream running through them.
Cleanliness:Dorito bags on the ground and broken glass on Umpire Rock.
Size:About 78,000 sq ft (1.8 acres). Central Park's largest playground.
Dad Test:Following the stream up Umpire Rock is fun for grown-ups too.
Bathrooms:Right in the playground, shared with the whole park.
From the full review

Heckscher is the OG, but not necessarily the GOAT. Known simply as “The Heck,” it's Central Park's oldest playground, opened in 1926, and at 1.8 acres, its largest. For my circle of parents, it's the default place for families to meet up. Everyone knows where it is, and everyone can find a spot.

The highlight is the castle-like stone structures with a little stream running through them. Following the stream from the drain all the way up to its source on Umpire Rock is fun for everyone, grown-ups included. You can even follow the stone causeway right up onto Umpire Rock itself, but I wouldn't recommend it for kids: there's usually broken glass up there from people drinking.

For some reason, my daughter's favorite part of this entire playground is a sewer drain just outside the sandbox. You can see water down below, and she'll stare into the abyss for ten minutes at a time. I wonder what she sees down there. Or what sees her.

Now for why we usually avoid it. Its central location in a very touristy area means it gets visited by all kinds of riff-raff. Even though it's huge, it's normally pretty darn busy. They call it “The Heck.” After the Saturday crowds, I have a few other names for it, but this is a family site. For some reason, it also attracts the kind of crowd that plays music out loud from their phones and leaves Dorito bags behind. During the week, it's full of nannies letting kids romp around. Keep an eye out, too: there are a few entrances and exits that kids can sneak out of. The bathrooms are attached to the playground, but they're shared with everyone in the park, so they can be pretty scuzzy.

Our most recent visit was a rainy Saturday in September, and the whole place was completely deserted, which was both fun and eerie. Verdict: still very much worth visiting for its sheer size and the view of the skyscrapers along Billionaires' Row. Just go on an off day if you can.

Billy Johnson Playground: The Secret Garden of Slides

Most Recently Reviewed On Sunday, September 13, 2026 | Filed under: Hidden Gems
Tucked out of sight from Fifth Avenue and the jogging loop, this rustic little playground is mostly locals, and plenty of parents don't know it exists. A granite slide, a storybook stone bridge with water jets underneath, and a tire swing that turns strangers into friends. A true hidden gem. Read the full review »
The Stump Report
Overall Fun:Granite slide, tire swing, and those sneaky bridge jets.
Originality:Nothing truly original, but the rustic stone-bridge setting is a charmer.
Cleanliness:Tidy and well tended.
Size:Approx. 20,000 sq ft, our estimate. Enough flat sandy space for a game of kickball.
Dad Test:Grown-ups fit the granite slide, and the bridge-jet prank never gets old.
Bathrooms:None nearby. Plan ahead.
From the full review

Here's one many Manhattan parents have walked right past. Billy Johnson Playground is tucked into Central Park near East 67th Street, and you can't see it from Fifth Avenue or from the jogging loop. I've mentioned it to plenty of fellow parents who had no idea it existed. If Heckscher is too busy or just not your style, this is the place to go — it's sort of the East Side's answer to the Adventure Playground on the West Side.

It was designed back in the 1980s to feel like a miniature version of the park around it, and it shows. There's no truly original equipment here, but there's a picturesque little stone bridge (a kid-sized cousin of Gapstow Bridge down at the Pond), a long granite slide set right into a rocky hill, and a nicer-than-average splash pad. Regulars bring a piece of cardboard to sit on for extra speed on the slide. Take the tip.

The best trick is under the bridge. Water jets are built into the pathway there, which makes a fun little stretch to run through over and over again. And there's always a certain kind of kid — I was one of them — who waits for some unsuspecting soul to stroll under the bridge, then hits the button and cackles as the jets go off. It never stops being funny.

The tire swing is where kids learn to share and make friends. There's also a nice flat, open sandy area that's perfect for a game of kickball. Line of sight isn't great with all the rocks and plantings, but there's only one entrance and exit, so there's nothing to worry about.

Verdict: a genuine hidden gem. I'm not sure I'd cross the city just for it, but if you're anywhere on the East Side, it's a lovely way to spend an afternoon.

Pier 6, Brooklyn Bridge Park: Four Playgrounds, One Pier

Most Recently Reviewed On Sunday, August 16, 2026 | Filed under: Worth the Trip & Most Fun for Parents
One of NYC's latest and greatest: a group of medium-sized playgrounds side by side that add up to an amusement park. A Water Lab built into a rocky hill (with an Archimedes screw!), steel slides, secret bamboo trails, and a serene swing valley. Very busy, but definitely worth a day trip. Read the full review »
The Stump Report
Overall Fun:Four playgrounds in one, like a little amusement park.
Originality:An Archimedes screw, bamboo trails, and a rocky-hill Water Lab.
Cleanliness:Well kept for how busy it gets.
Size:About 70,000 sq ft (1.6 acres). Four separate play zones to explore.
Dad Test:The Water Lab, steel slides, and plenty for grown-ups on the pier.
Bathrooms:On the pier by the Water Lab; a hike from the slides.
From the full review

Pier 6 is one of NYC's latest and greatest, and it isn't really one playground. It's a group of medium-sized playgrounds all next to each other — Slide Mountain, Swing Valley, Sandbox Village, and the Water Lab — that add up to an amusement park. Having separate zones feels more fun than one giant play area. It feels like an adventure going from one to the next and finding more fun stuff around every corner.

In the slide park, there are some steel slides that give Slide Hill a run for its money. The curl at the bottom of the pyramid slide looks totally rad. Unfortunately, the slide just doesn't generate enough speed to send kids around the berm, so they slowly slide to a stop and fall off the side. Still fun, just not as fun as it looks.

The impromptu trails through the bamboo in the slide park were super popular among kids, mine included. Nobody designed them; kids just made them, which is the best kind of playground feature. Swing Valley was more serene than fun, and the one exception to the crowds.

The splash pad, rebranded the “Water Lab,” was the busiest zone, and for good reason: it's unlike any other splash pad in the city. It's built into the side of a rocky hill, with lots of original water features, including an Archimedes screw that kids can crank to lift water uphill.

It was very busy when we visited on a weekend, with barely any room for parents to sit on the sidelines. This park is so far out there, I was surprised to find so many people. It's an interesting mix: some foreign tourists, some day-trippers from other parts of the city like us, and some locals. The bathrooms are on the pier nearest the Water Lab, which can be a bit of a hike from the slides on the other side.

Verdict: there's plenty on the pier for grown-ups too, so it's definitely worth making a day trip. Pro tip: bring snacks. Not for the kids. For negotiating with the kids. Bonus: the Governors Island ferry leaves from this same pier, so you can pair it with The Yard.

Pier 97 Playground: Needs a Better Maintenance Team

Pier 97 opened recently, right on the Hudson near 59th Street. The star is the big slide, which you can only reach by climbing a tall ropes course in the middle of the playground. There's always a dad shouting “Hudson!” or “Ava!” or “Jaxerton!” into a fish while his kid plays happily somewhere in the tail. For the record, parents do fit inside. But know going in that line of sight is limited, so this isn't the place to sit on a bench and scroll Instagram.

Around the sturgeon are more fun things: climbing nets, rubbery lumps, and misters that make a cloud of steam around some sculpted shrimp. The regular playground next door is pretty darn fun too. It has most of the usual stuff, including a splash pad, plus a delightfully off-kilter merry-go-round of sorts. There are serviceberry trees planted around, and in spring they have edible berries. Be prepared for weird looks, but they're delicious.

Right in the middle, between the fish and the other playground section, there's a public bathroom, which every parent will appreciate. On top of all that, there's a mini golf course, an oyster bar on a boat, and volleyball courts nearby, so the grown-ups are well looked after.

Verdict: worth the trip. The only downsides are that it's a little hard to get to, and it gets extremely busy on weekends. Go early.

From the full review

This is a little playground just for toddlers, right next to the Adventure Playground in Central Park. We came here a lot when my daughter was learning to walk, and honestly, this playground helped her take her first steps. That alone earns it a soft spot in my heart.

The whole ground is covered in soft rubber, with little lumps sticking up for toddlers to grab, pull themselves up on, and then try to toddle over to the next one. It's like a training course for walking. All the tots seem to instinctively make their way up the rubber mountain and perch at the top. Thankfully, they don't know how to play king of the hill yet.

There are also swings, a sandbox, and a water sprayer (I wouldn't call it a full splash pad). Since there's a bigger and more exciting playground right next door, there aren't any rowdy big kids running around here. Just toddlers. And lots of pacifiers.

Expect local Upper West Side parents calmly hanging out with babies from about six months to two years old. When nature calls, the bathrooms are in Tavern on the Green, a few hundred feet away.

Verdict: it suits its purpose perfectly, but don't expect anything too exciting. Once your little one is walking with confidence, graduate to the Adventure Playground next door.
